
## What Formalism stands for and its purpose

To understand why Formalism was important, it is imperative to look towards what literary criticism looked like before it. Before Formalist discipline of literary critiquing was a thing, external sources like the biography of an author, their moral and philosophical inclinations and the historical periods that shaped them. The literary work itself — its specific language, internal structure, individual creativity were rarely taken into account.

This resulted in an experiment involving a Cambridge critic, I.A. Richards, with several university students being provided with poems devoid of author names, titles, or dates that revealed any information about the content. The results revealed how hardly any student could identify what couplets/poems were intrinsically conveying.

The diagnosis was clear — contextualisation made understanding literature easier in lieu of compromising on the structural and intrinsic aspects of the work.

This is the problem Formalist criticism set out to solve.

## The Key figures who paved the road for Formalism

*A. Richards*, originally a psychologist as well as a critic, helped in identifying the core of the problem in literary criticism and proposed a definitive solution. Richards expanded on the idea of ambiguity in literature (a work having more than one potential meanings), shining a light on its positive quality. This was later revered by many New Critics as one of the productive elements of Formalism.

*T.S. Eliot*, one of the most prolific contributors in the Imagist genre of English literature, also wrote critical essays that contributed to Formalist criticism. His concept of “objective correlative” emphasises that art must find a way of expressing emotions indirectly through specific concrete images and situations. This is pertinent to Formalism in such a way that it helps embody emotions by embedding them within the work. His second approach, countering Biographical approach to criticism, focuses on the “impersonality” of the poetry where the poet says nothing about themselves per se but the experience the poem creates. This concept foreshadowed the Intentional Fallacy argument of Formalism.

*John Ransom*, a contributor in the nomenclature of New Criticism, was interested in the specific contribution of the poetic texture (the local irreducible details of poetic language) to meanings that could not be expressed by the poem’s logical structure.

Cleanth Brooks pioneered an approach to close reading. One of his concepts emphasizes that the meaning of the content and its formalistic structure are inseparable. The second concept is the account of paradox as the language of poetry — the statement that appears self-contradictory but reveals a deeper truth on examination. He claims it to be a mode of reaching truth, saying what cannot be said directly.

## The Autotelic text

An autotelic text is a text that is valuable for its own sake, rather than for some other purpose.

In the easiest language possible:

- Auto = self
- Telic = purpose or goal

So, an autotelic text is a text whose purpose is found within itself.

Example: A poem that you read simply because you enjoy its beauty, language, rhythm, and meaning is autotelic.

Remember the difference:

- Autotelic text: You read it for the pleasure of reading it.
- Practical text: You read it to get information or do something, such as reading a user manual to learn how to operate a phone.

In literary studies, the term often means that a literary work is appreciated as a work of art in itself, not merely as a tool for teaching a lesson or conveying information.

One-line definition for your exam:

“An autotelic text is a literary work that has its purpose and value within itself and is appreciated as an independent work of art.”

## Organic Form and the oneness of form and content

Organic form was borrowed from the Romantic tradition. It basically means that, unlike Mechanical form that is formed by the imposition of outside forces, a literary work is one in which every element — every word, every image, every structural choice, every rhythmic decision — participates in the work’s total meaning as a whole. Nothing is placed by accident, nothing is purely decorative, and nothing is surplus. Every part serves the whole and is shaped by the whole.

This further implies that, if form and content are one and inseparable, then paraphrasing the poem can completely change its construct and meaning. The paraphrase is not the poem; it’s a different literary work altogether.

## Close Reading

Close reading represents the actual application of everything that has been mentioned theoretically up to this point. Close reading involves the ability to look at what a piece of literature actually does with language, and how words are used, images created, rhythms arranged, syntax managed, and meanings made through connections between all the parts.

## Texture, Image, Symbol: The Building Blocks of Literary Meaning

### *Texture*

Texture is a term Ransom used to describe the local, detailed, irreducible quality of poetic language— the specific feel of the words themselves as distinct from the poem’s logical argument or narrative structure.

---

### *Image*

Image, in the literary sense, is a specific sensory detail in a text — something that a reader can imaginatively see, hear, smell, taste, or feel.

---

### *Symbol*

A symbol is what an image acquires as more meaning is added to it aside from its literal representation.
