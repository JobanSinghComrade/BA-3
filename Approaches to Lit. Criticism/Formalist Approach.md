## What Formalism stands for and its purpose

To understand why Formalism was important, it is imperative to look towards what literary criticism looked like before it. Before Formalist discipline of literary critiquing was a thing, external sources like the biography of an author, their moral and philosophical inclinations and the historical periods that shaped them. The literary work itself — its specific language, internal structure, individual creativity were rarely taken into account. 

This resulted in an experiment involving a Cambridge critic I.A. Richards with a number of university students being provided with poems devoid of author names, titles, or dates that revealed any information about the content. The results revealed how hardly any student could identify what couplets/poems were intrinsically conveying.

The diagnosis was clear — contextualisation made understanding literature easier in lieu of compromising on the structural and intrinsic aspects of the work. 

This is the problem Formalist criticism set out to solve.

## The Key figures who paved the road for Formalism

A. Richards, originally a psychologist as well as a critic, helped in identifying the core of the problem in literary criticism and proposed a definitive solution. Richards expanded on the idea ambiguity of literature (a work having more than one potential meanings), shining a light on its positive quality. This was later revered by many New Critics as one of the productive elements of Formalism.

T.S. Eliot, one of the most prolific contributors in the Imagist genre of English literature, also wrote critical essays that contributed to the Formalist criticism. His concept of "objective correlative" emphasises that art must find a way of expression of emotions indirectly through specific concrete images and situations. This is pertinent to Formalism in such way that it helps embody emotions by embedding them within the work. His second approach, countering Biographical approach to criticism, focuses on "impersonality" of the poetry where the poet says nothing about themselves per se but the experience the poem creates. This concept foreshadowed the Intentional Fallacy argument of Formalism.
