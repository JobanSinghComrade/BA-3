#### Types:
# Historical approach:

Historical approach to literary criticism studies a literary work in relation to the historical period, social conditions, political events, cultural values, and the intellectual climate it was produced during. Every literary work reflects the historical context, author's background, customs, traditions religion, literary traditions, and economic conditions. However, it should not be reduced to a only historical document. Rather, it is a creative expression shaped by its surrounding conditions. Hippolyte Taine (1828–1893), the scientific spirit of Darwin's era, was a French critic and historian who tried to look towards making literary criticism as solid as the natural sciences. He felt existing criticism was not as clear and that it either praised or negatively criticized works without explaining _why_ particular literature emerged in particular times and places. His answer was a theory of three forces acting all at the same time on every writer during any given period:

- **Race** — not biological, but a nation's deep-rooted cultural character, built up over centuries through shared history, geography, religion, and social structure; *what you inherit from your background* (e.g., a writer raised in Punjabi cultural environment influenced to write about family honour, village life, community and traditions, etc).
- **Milieu** — the immediate social, political, economic, and intellectual environment a writer lives and works in; *the environment around you* (e.g., the religious and political conditions of Elizabethan England, or colonial Bengal's influence on Tagore).
- **Moment** — the existing state of literary tradition when a writer begins working — the accumulated conventions, genres, and possibilities inherited from earlier writers (e.g., how 1820s poets wrote in the wake of Wordsworth and Byron, how practice of Sufism influenced Punjabi writers like Baba Fareed, Bulleh Shah, Waris Shah, Shah Hussain, Sultan bahu, etc.)

Taine argued literature is "not a mere play of imagination," rejecting Romantic notions (from Coleridge, Wordsworth, etc.) that treated creativity as near-divine and hard to explain. Instead, he insisted literary works arise from identifiable historical conditions that can be traced and tracked.
The historical approach applies the three forces of Taine to _Hamlet_ (English Protestant Christianity perspective, mixed Elizabethan religious traditions to keep the peace between the Protestant and Catholic Christians), _Paradise Lost_ (Milton's Protestant moral seriousness, his infuences of epic tradition of Homer and Virgil, etc.), and R.K. Narayan's _The Guide_ (Tamil Brahmin/Hindu philosophical upbringing, India's social tensions after partition, and the emerging Indian English literature). The forces mentioned above are also clearly visible in works like Bhagat Singh's *Why I Am An Atheist* (Colonial India deeply rooted in religious dogma, superstitions and polarized against one another), Niccolo Machiavelli's *The Prince* with politically divided and turbulent Italy, period of Renassance writing-style and deep involvement of the Church(religion) with the State(governance) which led to the *The Prince* becoming a taboo and even banning of the book. The forces also shine a light on the treatment of certain creeds and classes of society during certain periods. For example, the Salem Witch trials which burned women alive in Europe who were accused of witchcraft in early 1690s as compared to the feminist literature that gradually started to rise in Victorian period to bring to attention humanitarian rights of women as they gained further momentum in the Modernist period that followed after 1900s. 

Critics have noted that Taine's approach to criticism is too deterministic, overgeneralizes human nature and gives little importance to individual creativity. Nonetheless, the three forces set the conditions for writing — the raw material available — but don't fully determine individual work; genius is what a writer does with those conditions beyond what they alone could produce (for e.g., Shakespeare vs. less popular Elizabethan dramatists).

# Biographical Approach


Biographical criticism is a way to study books and literary work. The idea is simple: writers are people. They have feelings. They have relationships. They have hard experiences in life. These things go into their writing. We can see small parts of a writer's life inside their books.

But this is not the same as gossip. Gossip is easy. It just talks about a person's life for fun. Biographical criticism is different. It needs discipline. A critic must show clear links. They must connect one fact from a writer's life to one part in the text. They cannot just say "this is interesting" and stop there.

This method also has a loopholes. Writers use imagination. They change their real life a lot when they write. So sometimes a critic's ideas show more about the critic than about the writer. Good biographical criticism is careful. It uses a writer's life to help us understand the text better. But it does not replace reading the text itself.

John Keats's "Ode to a Nightingale" explores how Keats took care of his brother Tom, who was dying of tuberculosis. Then Keats saw the same sickness in his own body. So when the poem talks about fever and tiredness, this is not just poetic language. It is real and medical. His wish for death is not really about wanting to die. It is about wanting rest from suffering he already saw which is a form of escapism. Charlotte Brontë wrote Jane Eyre. She had feelings for her married teacher in Brussels, Constantin Heger. But the book is not a direct copy of her life. Rochester is not Heger. Thornfield is not Brussels. Still, her life helps us understand strong feelings in the book, like Jane's self-control and the story's message about equal love between partners. Sylvia Plath wrote The Bell Jar. The story is very close to her real life: a work internship, a mental breakdown, a suicide attempt, and time in hospital. It is so close that she first used a fake name to publish it. But the book is special because of its style. There is emotional distance between the character and the narrator's voice. This is a skill that biography alone cannot explain. Kamala Das wrote poems in English. She herself connected her life and her writing in her book My Story. Her poems talk about her early arranged marriage and her loneliness. She turns her personal pain into bigger ideas about women's lives. Her poems use images of feeling trapped, connected to her culture and home.

Next, there is a big argument against this method. It is called the "Intentional Fallacy," by Wimsatt and Beardsley. They say a writer's life and intentions do not matter for understanding a text. Only the text itself matters.

Biographical criticism is not a rigid benchmark of critiquing. Instead, it says both methods work together. Biography shows what the writer brought to the work. Formal analysis shows what the writer built from it. Together, they help us understand a text more deeply, without replacing the art of the text itself.
