# Historical approach:

Historical approach to literary criticism studies a literary work in relation to the historical period, social conditions, political events, cultural values, and the intellectual climate it was producing during. Every literary work reflects the historical context, author's background, customs, traditions religion, literary traditions, and economics, but should not be reduced to a mere historical document; rather, it's a creative expression shaped by its surrounding conditions. Hippolyte Taine (1828–1893), influenced by Auguste Comte's positivism and the scientific spirit of Darwin's era, was a French critic and historian who sought to make literary criticism as rigorous as the natural sciences. He felt existing criticism was too vague, praising or condemning works without explaining _why_ particular literature emerged in particular times and places. His answer was a theory of three forces acting simultaneously on every writer:

- **Race** — not biological, but a nation's deep-rooted cultural character, built up over centuries through shared history, geography, religion, and social structure (e.g., the moral seriousness and introspection Taine attributed to English Protestant culture).
- **Milieu** — the immediate social, political, economic, and intellectual environment a writer lives and works in (e.g., the religious and political conditions of Elizabethan England, or colonial Bengal's influence on Tagore).
- **Moment** — the existing state of literary tradition when a writer begins working — the accumulated conventions, genres, and possibilities inherited from earlier writers (e.g., how 1820s poets wrote in the wake of Wordsworth and Byron).

Taine argued literature is "not a mere play of imagination," rejecting Romantic notions (from Coleridge, Wordsworth, Shelley) that treated creativity as near-divine and inexplicable. Instead, he insisted literary works arise from identifiable, traceable historical conditions. Other critics in the history of literature, for example Saint-Beuve, also emphasized the writer's life, personality, and historical environment in understanding what influences literary works took to manifest.  
The historical approach applies the three forces of Taine to _Hamlet_ (English Protestant introspection, Elizabethan religious ambiguity, the Revenge Tragedy genre), _Paradise Lost_ (Milton's Protestant moral seriousness, his post-Restoration political disgrace, and his engagement with the epic tradition of Homer and Virgil), and R.K. Narayan's _The Guide_ (Tamil Brahmin/Hindu philosophical inheritance, post-independence India's social tensions, and the emerging Indian English literary voice). The aforementioned forces are also discernible in works like Bhagat Singh's *Why I Am An Atheist*(Colonial India deeply rooted in religious dogma and polarized against one another), Niccolo Machiavelli's *The Prince*(politically fragmented and turbulent Italy, period of Renassance writing-style and deep involvement of the Church with the state which led to the *The Prince* becoming a taboo and even banning of the book). The forces also shine a light on the treatment of certain creeds and classes during certain periods. For example, the Salem Witch trials that burned women in Europe accused of witchcraft in early 1690s, juxtaposed to the feminist literature that gradually started to rise in Victorian period and gained further momentum in the Modernist period that followed. 

Nonetheless, the three forces set the conditions for writing — the raw material available — but don't fully determine individual achievement; genius is what a writer does with those conditions beyond what they alone could produce (for e.g., Shakespeare vs. lesser Elizabethan dramatists).

Historical criticism offers empirical grounding, guards against anachronistic misreading, and gives critics a structured method. However, Taine's "race" concept risks overgeneralization, historical criticism can substitute history for literary analysis, it struggles to explain why old works remain powerful today, and later approaches (New Historicism, Cultural Materialism, Postcolonialism) have challenged its assumption of a unified historical "moment."
