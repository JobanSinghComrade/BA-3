## Freudian concept of the unconscious or the subconscious

 Freud’s map divides the mind into **conscious, preconscious, and unconscious/subconscious**. The conscious holds current awareness, the preconscious stores retrievable memories, while the unconscious contains repressed desires, memories, and instincts influencing behaviour. According to **Sigmund Freud**, the conscious mind contains thoughts and feelings we are aware of. The preconscious contains memories that can be brought into awareness. One could liken this to the main act of a play that is visible to the audience. The **unconscious** is the deepest part of the mind and contains repressed desires, fears, memories, and instincts that we are not directly aware of. This part of one's psyche could be compared to all that is happening behind the curtain when the play is going on. Freud believed that unconscious forces strongly influence our behaviour, emotions, dreams, and decisions. Repressed wishes may appear indirectly through **dreams, slips of the tongue, fantasies, and neurotic symptoms**. Thus, much of human behaviour is shaped by hidden mental processes.

## The divisions of the mind: Id, Ego and Superego

Freud had a second model of the mind that was developed in the 1920s that divided the mind into 3 understandable parts: the Id, the Ego and the Superego. The Id, considered the most primal part of the psyche, is the part that the 'self' really is at the core. It is the core instinctual energy that is devoid of any sense of morality, time, patience or fear of consequence. Basically, it is any drive, desire or form of passion that is beyond the point of rational control. Some people, outside of psychological jargon, also describe it as one's animalistic self or 'soul'. The Ego is said to have formed after the Id, and it is described as the part that deals with reality and mediates between one's Id's demands and the constraints of the world. Ego opertes on the principles of external reality. The Superego, being considered the last of the three agencies of the mind to form, is most easily understood as the part of the self that emerges out of a state of metacognition; the state of being able to think about one's thoughts or thinking "beyond" one's thoughts and the intrinsic significance of the consequences of the thoughts. It is the part that says "you should not want to do that".

## Repression and its inevitable emergence

The concept of repression forms quite the foundation for most of the entire psychoanalytic theory. Repression does not happen voluntarily. It happens below the level of awareness. It is something that the Ego detects as stemming from the Id's content and as something that would cause an insurmountable level of anxiety to the self, eventually, if it were allowed to reach consciousness. The repression is largely unknown to the person, and only its indirect results are known. The indirect results are what Freud calls the 'return' of the repressed. The repressed is what manifests in the an individual's dreams as certain symbols and imagery and people in their dreams. It also happens to manifest outwardly in the form of art through writing, music, films, painting, dancing, etc., sometimes literally and sometimes in the form of symbolic imagery.

## Dream-work: How the Unconscious Disguises Itself in the Form of Imagery

Sigmund Freud’s concept of **dream-work** is an important part of his theory of the unconscious mind. Freud believed that dreams are not meaningless collections of images. Instead, they are expressions of **unconscious wishes, desires, fears, memories, and conflicts**. However, these unconscious thoughts cannot usually appear directly in a dream because they may be unacceptable, threatening, or disturbing to the conscious mind. Therefore, the unconscious disguises them through a psychological process called **dream-work**.

Dream-work transforms the **latent content** of a dream—the hidden thoughts and wishes—into its **manifest content**, which is the dream as the person remembers it. According to Freud, several processes are involved in this transformation.

These processes include **condensation**, **displacement**, **symbolization**, etc. In condensation, several different ideas, people, memories, or emotions are combined into a single dream image. For example, a person in a dream may have the appearance of one individual but the personality or characteristics of several others. A single image can therefore represent many different unconscious thoughts at once. Through displacement, emotional importance is transferred from an important or threatening idea to a less threatening object. A person may unconsciously feel anger toward someone but dream about being angry with a completely different person or object. In this way, the true source of the emotion is disguised. Freud argued that, with symbolic imagery, unconscious wishes, particularly sexual or aggressive impulses, may be represented by symbols rather than appearing directly. Objects, places, actions, or situations in dreams may therefore have meanings beyond their literal appearance. For example, a journey might symbolize psychological change, while certain enclosed spaces might represent aspects of the self. However, Freud did not believe that every symbol had one universal meaning; its interpretation depended on the dreamer’s personal associations.

For Freud, studying these transformations provides a way of gaining access to the unconscious and understanding the conflicts and desires that influence human behaviour.

## Freud's Defence Mechanisms 

**Defence mechanisms** are unconscious ways our mind protects us from **anxiety, fear, guilt, embarrassment, or uncomfortable thoughts**.

Sigmund Freud believed that when reality becomes emotionally difficult, the mind may automatically use these mechanisms to reduce the discomfort.

Here are the main ones, **one by one**:

### 1. Repression

**Meaning:** Pushing an uncomfortable thought or memory out of conscious awareness.

**Example:**  
A person experiences something very painful and later cannot remember it clearly.

👉 **Simple idea:** _“I don't want to think about this, so my mind pushes it away.”_

---

### 2. Denial

**Meaning:** Refusing to accept something that is actually happening.

**Example:**  
Someone is told they have a serious problem but says, _“No, there's nothing wrong with me.”_

👉 **Simple idea:** _“This isn't happening.”_

---

### 3. Projection

**Meaning:** Putting your own unacceptable feelings or thoughts onto someone else.

**Example:**  
A person who is jealous of a friend accuses the friend of being jealous of them.

👉 **Simple idea:** _“It's not me who feels this—it's you.”_

---

### 4. Displacement

**Meaning:** Taking your emotions out on a safer or less threatening person or object.

**Example:**  
Your boss makes you angry, but you cannot argue with your boss, so you go home and become angry with your family.

👉 **Simple idea:** _“I'm angry at A, but I take it out on B.”_

---

### 5. Rationalization

**Meaning:** Creating a logical-sounding explanation for something we did or something that happened, instead of accepting the real reason.

**Example:**  
A student fails an exam and says, _“I didn't really want to pass anyway.”_

👉 **Simple idea:** _“I'll give myself a better excuse.”_

---

### 6. Regression

**Meaning:** Returning to behaviour associated with an earlier stage of life when under stress.

**Example:**  
An adult becomes unusually childish, cries easily, or throws a tantrum when facing extreme stress.

👉 **Simple idea:** _“I go back to an earlier way of behaving.”_

---

### 7. Sublimation

**Meaning:** Turning an unacceptable or socially problematic impulse into something useful or socially acceptable.

**Example:**  
Someone with a lot of anger channels it into boxing or intense exercise.

👉 **Simple idea:** _“I turn a difficult impulse into something productive.”_

---

### 8. Reaction Formation

**Meaning:** Behaving in the opposite way to what you actually feel.

**Example:**  
Someone strongly dislikes another person but behaves extremely friendly toward them.

👉 **Simple idea:** _“I feel one thing, but I act like I feel the opposite.”_

---

### 9. Intellectualization

**Meaning:** Focusing on facts and logic instead of experiencing the emotional side of a painful situation.

**Example:**  
After a breakup, someone talks endlessly about the psychology and statistics of relationships but avoids talking about their own sadness.

👉 **Simple idea:** _“I'll think about it instead of feeling it.”_

---

### 10. Identification

**Meaning:** Adopting the characteristics, behaviour, or attitudes of someone you admire or feel threatened by.

**Example:**  
A shy person begins copying the confidence and mannerisms of someone they admire.

👉 **Simple idea:** _“I'll become more like that person.”_

---

### 11. Introjection

**Meaning:** Taking another person's beliefs, attitudes, or values and making them part of yourself.

**Example:**  
A child repeatedly hears, _“You must always be perfect,”_ and eventually develops that belief as their own.

👉 **Simple idea:** _“Their voice becomes part of my inner voice.”_

---

### 12. Avoidance

**Meaning:** Staying away from situations, people, thoughts, or feelings that cause anxiety.

**Example:**  
Someone who fears public speaking repeatedly avoids situations where they would have to speak.

👉 **Simple idea:** _“If I stay away from it, I won't feel anxious.”_

---

## 🧠 The easiest way to remember them

**In one sentence:** Freud's defence mechanisms are basically **the mind's automatic ways of protecting itself from psychological discomfort.**


## Where Psychoanalytic Criticism is conducive in literary research 

Psychoanalytic approach sees beyond what is apparent in the literary work given. It emphasizes what is not visible but nonetheless present. It shines light on the subliminal aspects of the psyche behind the character or the author, which is most likely missed in a formal or historical approach. It also tends to illustrate well the emotional mechanisms behind literature and art, such as the idea that aesthetic form lowers the defences of the mind and allows access to otherwise blocked emotional material. Psychoanalytic criticism also provides a painstaking explanation for irrational human behaviours that could otherwise have been taken literally without any two cents thrown in as to why they're being indulged in. 

## Where Psychoanalytic Criticism falls short 

Rigidity becomes a problem where a certain agency of the mind is used to generalize the individuality of a character. For example, not everything is a source of a man's Oedipus complex or a woman's Electra complex. Human nature is far too ornate and intricate to put in a generalizing box of rigid behaviours. With this, the problem of the lack of empirical and forensic evidence always exists. In many cases, psychoanalytic criticism becomes an example of cognitive bias and misinterpretation. Misalignment and disagreement within many literary factions make things more complicated for reaching a conclusion set in stone. Most importantly, reaching a concluding the right motif in a literary work becomes the least feasible because of the lack of access to the canonical author's original space of mind where the work originated from.
