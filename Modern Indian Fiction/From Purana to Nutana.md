## Summary: "Purana to Nutana" 


This chapter opens Meenakshi Mukherjee's _Realism and Reality_, examining how the novel emerged in India as both a literary and socio-political phenomenon. The author insists the topic can't be treated as pure literary history: the novel's rise in India after the mid-nineteenth century stemmed jointly from colonial conditions and from older, mutated indigenous narrative traditions. It's telling that the first Indian novels (in Bengali and Marathi) appeared roughly a generation after Macaulay's Educational Minutes made English central to Indian schooling—yet treating the novel as simply a British import, like railways or cricket, would ignore deeper cultural forces.

**Section I** surveys Western theories of the novel's origins—its links to the rise of the bourgeoisie/capitalism and to emerging individualism—then questions how well these frameworks transfer to India, given that prose narrative existed there far earlier (e.g., Banabhatta's _Kadambari_) without industrialization or a middle class. Since the novel resists tidy definition, Mukherjee instead outlines contrasts with pre-novel narrative forms: the novel's structure is comparatively linear and organically unified versus the circular, nested, or chain-like structure of older tales (Kadambari, Panchatantra, Arabian Nights, Decameron); the novel is anchored to concrete historical and geographical coordinates rather than a timeless "once upon a time" setting; and its characters are individualized rather than stylized archetypes of romance or fable.

**Section II** turns to the practical difficulties nineteenth-century Indian writers faced adapting European realism to Indian life. Popular Victorian writers (Wilkie Collins, Disraeli, Reynolds, Corelli, Bulwer Lytton) were often more influential than Dickens or Thackeray. The central tension was reconciling the novel's requirement for individual choice and agency with a rigidly hierarchical, tradition-bound society where marriage and profession were communal rather than personal decisions. Mukherjee illustrates this through prefaces by Marathi novelist Naro Sadashiv Risbud (who abandons realism as too dull) and Malayalam novelist O. Chandu Menon, whose _Indulekha_ projects an idealized, freer heroine into an imagined future rather than depicting existing society. The chapter also traces how the novel's demand for a "steadfast consciousness of the present" and linear historical time diverged from the cyclical, mythic time of puranic and kavya traditions—though this Western influence dominated conscious models more than unconscious ones.

**Section III** examines the varied indigenous terms coined for "novel"—Bengali _upanyas_, Marathi _kadambari_, Urdu _naval_, Gujarati _naval-katha_, Telugu _vachana prabandha_—showing these differences mattered less than the shared aspiration to emulate English fiction. It notes the crucial role of British officials and missionaries in patronizing, prize-giving, translating, and even textbook-prescribing early novels, alongside authors' own didactic or reformist motives (Nazir Ahmad, Samuel Vedanayakam Pillai).

**Section IV** highlights the late arrival of prose literature generally in Indian languages (verse dominated even scientific and legal texts until the early 1800s), meaning novelists were simultaneously pioneering both a new genre and a barely-tested prose medium. Mukherjee closes by suggesting the British Victorian model may have been a poor fit compared to Russian or American alternatives, yet despite servile imitation (Chandu Menon adapting Disraeli, Apte translating Reynolds, Bankim dubbed "the Scott of Bengal"), the Indian novel gradually developed its own distinctive character—setting up the book's broader project of tracing that synthesis.
