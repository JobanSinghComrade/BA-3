A couple of years after the Partition of India, the governments of India and Pakistan decide that lunatic asylum inmates, like prisoners, should also be exchanged along religious lines — Muslim lunatics from India sent to Pakistan, and Hindu and Sikh lunatics from Pakistan sent to India. The story unfolds mainly in the Lahore asylum, where news of this decision causes confusion and absurd debate among the inmates, most of whom have no real grasp of what Partition means or where the new borders lie.

Manto populates the asylum with a gallery of characters whose madness ironically mirrors the irrationality of Partition itself. One inmate climbs a tree, refusing to live in either India or Pakistan, insisting he'll live "in this tree" instead. Two men declare themselves Muhammad Ali Jinnah and Master Tara Singh respectively, prompting the authorities to separate them to prevent communal violence. A Hindu lawyer, driven mad by heartbreak, mourns that his beloved in Amritsar has now become "Indian" while he becomes "Pakistani." Two Anglo-Indian inmates fret anxiously over their uncertain status now that the British are leaving. Through these vignettes, Manto satirizes how the political division of the subcontinent scrambled identity, belonging, and reason itself — sane logic looks as absurd as madness once nations are redrawn overnight.

At the center of the story is Bishan Singh, a Sikh landlord from the town of Toba Tek Singh, who has been confined for fifteen years and is known by the name of his hometown. He never sleeps, stands constantly on swollen legs, and speaks only in a nonsensical string of syllables ending in phrases about "the government of Pakistan" or whatever authority is currently on his mind. Once he had a daughter who visited him, and he retained a strange instinctive sense of when she was coming — but the visits stopped once communal unrest began, deepening his disorientation. When he hears of the exchange, his single, obsessive question becomes: is Toba Tek Singh located in India or in Pakistan? No one can give him a satisfactory answer, and even the other inmates and staff offer only contradictory or mocking replies.

Shortly before the transfer, an old friend named Fazal Din visits him — the first visitor in fifteen years — and tells him that his family has safely reached India and that his daughter is fine. Bishan Singh barely reacts, still fixated on the location of his home.

On the day of the exchange, chaos engulfs the asylum as inmates are forced onto buses bound for the Wagha border. When Bishan Singh is asked to give his name for the record, he again asks where Toba Tek Singh is; told it is now in Pakistan, he refuses to cross into India. Guards try to force him over the line, but he plants himself in no-man's-land, between the two barbed-wire fences, and will not be moved. He is eventually left alone to stand there through the night. Just before sunrise, he lets out a scream and collapses, dying on that unclaimed strip of earth — belonging to neither nation.

The story ends with Manto's haunting final image: behind one line of barbed wire lies India, behind the other lies Pakistan, and in between, on a nameless patch of ground, lies Toba Tek Singh — a devastating metaphor for the human cost and absurdity of Partition, where identity and homeland are severed by arbitrary lines.

### **Themes Explored**

The major themes of _Toba Tek Singh_ include **the absurdity and tragedy of Partition, displacement and loss of homeland, fractured identity and belonging, religious communalism, the arbitrariness of political borders, the human cost of nationalism, madness versus sanity, alienation and homelessness, loss and trauma, uncertainty over national identity, and the futility of bureaucratic and political authority**. Through the asylum and its inmates, Manto ironically suggests that the supposedly “sane” political world outside may be more irrational than the world of the mentally ill.
